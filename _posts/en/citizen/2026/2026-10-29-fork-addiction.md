Picture tomorrow morning's front-page headlines:

> Seasonal fruit, fresh vegetables, or dripping burgers: the average person spends over three hours a day eating. Shock investigation into the scourge of "fork addiction."

Or even:

> History podcasts, symphonic music, conspiracy debates: we spend five hours a day glued to our speakers and headphones. Is it time to ban vibrating membranes?

Absurd? Of course it is.

Yet that is precisely the depth of analysis we are treated to whenever digital technology is discussed. For years, mainstream media, pundits, and politicians have brandished the exact same bogeyman: "screen addiction."

A hollow, convenient, and profoundly depoliticized buzzword.

## Confusing the Vessel with the Content

The first flaw is technical and semantic: blaming the tool instead of the activity makes no sense.

A screen is nothing more than a photon receiver, a backlit panel—just as a transistor or Wi-Fi are merely physical conduits for information. Claiming someone is "hooked on screens" makes as much sense as diagnosing an "addiction to paper" on the grounds that we indifferently print sci-fi novels, tax bills, poetry, grocery flyers, or far-right propaganda tracts on it.

Lumping every single use under the catch-all banner of "screen time" conveniently erases all nuance. In those panic-inducing statistics of multiple hours a day, what are we actually tallying?

- Work hours (where people are explicitly required to sit in front of a monitor for eight hours a day);
- Everyday commerce: booking train tickets, ordering a book, click-and-collect grocery shopping, comparing energy quotes, paying for a gym membership;
- Paperless administrative procedures;
- Private conversations with family, friends, communities, colleagues, or comrades;
- Creating, learning, reading;
- Consuming cultural content, news, and entertainment;
- And, finally, passively scrolling algorithmic feeds.

Lumping together an hour of coding, an hour on video call with your grandmother, and an hour of mindless doomscrolling on TikTok as one single pathological behavior is pure intellectual laziness.

And frankly, nobody is truly fooled. Whenever people are asked about "screen addiction," they invariably bring up video games and social networks. They never talk about the hours they spend reading articles, watching movies, or following sewing tutorials on YouTube.

## Addiction is a Serious Matter—Let's Treat It Seriously

Let's be clear: I am not dismissing the reality of psychological distress or the mechanics of dependency. Addressing addiction is vital. Real people—both young and older—are losing sleep, becoming isolated, and suffering.

But addiction belongs to the medical and psychological realm. It demands precise clinical definitions, identifiable symptoms, and appropriate therapeutic support.

When someone experiences distress tied to their digital habits, their need for help is entirely legitimate. However, slapping the label "screen addict" onto that distress conceals the true source of the problem. It fixates on a crude, visible symptom rather than investigating the root causes.

And that is entirely by design.

## The Attention Economy and Predatory Design: How the Trap Works

What we are up against is not some fetishistic obsession with a small, glowing rectangle (unless we're talking about my love for the Triforce, but that's another story). We are up against digital products engineered with surgical precision to capture and hold our attention for profit.

Screens do not make people addicted. What does is:

- Recommendation algorithms, fine-tuned to trigger outrage, anxiety, or variable intermittent rewards (the classic "slot machine" effect).
- Predatory interfaces riddled with dark patterns: infinite scroll, autoplay, deceptive push notifications, and worse.
- The advertising business model, which converts every single second of our available brain time into monetizable ad inventory.

When you lose 45 minutes without realizing it, watching an endless loop of absurd videos, it isn't some mysterious failure of willpower in the face of a black mirror: it is the direct outcome of entire teams of engineers and designers paid top dollar to exploit our cognitive biases.

And when researchers blow the whistle and rigorously document these predatory practices, what do the platforms do? They unleash armies of lobbyists to discredit independent science, pit academics against one another, and bury inconvenient findings[^meta].

## Who Benefits from the Fog?

Why this stubborn refusal to call things by their name? Why this refusal to dissect what actually fills those hours?

Because scrutinizing usage means asking a political question: who owns the platforms whose algorithmic editorial choices shape our information diet and, by extension, our lives? Billionaires locked in an arms race of lobbying to guarantee nothing threatens their bottom line.

So instead, we talk about "screen addiction." We dump the entire burden of responsibility onto the individual (or onto supposedly negligent parents). We moralize, we guilt-trip, we hand out tips for "digital detoxes" and app screen-time limits.

Meanwhile, we conveniently avoid the core issues:

- Why do we allow predatory design mechanics to thrive without strict regulation?
- Why aren't we challenging the economic model of surveillance advertising?
- Why aren't amplification algorithms regulated the same way we enforce food safety and hygiene standards?

If your food is laced with addictive or toxic chemicals, your fork isn't the problem. The problem is the manufacturer poisoning the plate and the regulator letting them get away with it. It is high time we dropped the moral panic over screens and started collectively, politically regulating what goes onto them.
